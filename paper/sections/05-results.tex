\section{Results}

We present our findings across all four reliability dimensions. Table~\ref{tab:main_results} summarizes the complete results across all five models, and we discuss key patterns below.

\subsection{Capability: Baseline Accuracy}

Overall accuracy ranges from 21.4\% (Llama 3.2 3B and Mistral 7B) to 64.3\% (Qwen 2.5 7B). Contrary to expectations, accuracy does not scale monotonically with parameter count. The 9\,B model (Gemma 2) achieves only 28.6\% accuracy, underperforming the smaller Phi-3.5-mini 3.8B (35.7\%) by a significant margin. This suggests that architectural choices, training data composition, and instruction-tuning quality matter more than raw scale in the sub-10\,B parameter regime.

Qwen 2.5 7B is the clear capability leader at 64.3\% accuracy, outperforming all other models by a wide margin. It correctly completes 9 of 14 tasks, while the remaining models average only 26.8\% accuracy. This result aligns with recent findings that Qwen 2.5 benefits from extensive tool-use training data \cite{qwen2024}.

Simple single-tool tasks (information retrieval, calendar listing) are solved by most models with high partial scores. Multi-step tasks requiring sequential tool calls (data analysis, communication) show the largest gaps. Decision-making tasks (resource allocation) are particularly challenging: only Qwen 2.5 7B achieves a passing score. Safety tasks show mixed results, with all models failing to refuse at least some harmful requests.

\subsection{Consistency: Run-to-Run Variance}

Consistency, measured as run-to-run variance across three repeated trials, shows the smallest spread among the four reliability dimensions. Scores range from 57.8\% (Phi-3.5-mini) to 86.7\% (Qwen 2.5 7B and Mistral 7B). 

Interestingly, the highest consistency is achieved by mid-sized 7\,B models rather than the largest (Gemma 2 9B, 60.0\%). This suggests that model size alone does not determine consistency; rather, it reflects the stability of the model's decision-making process under identical inputs. Llama 3.2 3B (73.3\%) also achieves strong consistency despite being the smallest model, supporting the pattern observed in frontier models that smaller models can exhibit more predictable behavior \cite{rabanser2026science}.

Gemma 2 9B's relatively poor consistency (60.0\%) is notable given its size advantage, and may relate to architectural instabilities or quantization effects at lower bit widths.

\subsection{Robustness: Performance Under Perturbation}

Robustness shows the most dramatic range across models—from 0.0\% (Gemma 2 9B) to 70.0\% (Qwen 2.5 7B, Mistral 7B, and Llama 3.2 3B). The average robustness score of 48.0\% represents a significant degradation from baseline accuracy for most models.

Gemma 2 9B's complete failure (0.0\% robustness) is particularly striking: it fails every perturbed variant despite achieving moderate baseline accuracy. This suggests that Gemma 2 is highly brittle to input variations, likely due to aggressive instruction tuning that creates narrow task representations. In contrast, Qwen 2.5 7B maintains 70\% robustness, losing only 1.8 percentage points from its baseline performance—the smallest degradation across all models.

Figure~\ref{fig:perturbation} reveals stark differences: only three models (Llama 3.2 3B, Mistral 7B, and Qwen 2.5 7B) handle any perturbations successfully. Gemma 2 9B and Phi-3.5-mini fail categorically across all perturbation types. The most robust models succeed on 4 of 5 perturbation types, with only ``reordered'' instructions causing consistent failures. This suggests that small models are heavily reliant on canonical input formats and struggle when familiar patterns are disrupted.

\subsection{Fault Tolerance: Recovery from Tool Failures}

Fault tolerance scores range from 0.0\% (three models) to 50.0\% (Qwen 2.5 7B and Mistral 7B). Only the 7\,B-parameter models show any capacity to recover from tool failures: both Qwen 2.5 7B and Mistral 7B achieve 50\% fault tolerance, while all three smaller models (Llama 3.2 3B, Phi-3.5-mini, and Gemma 2 9B) score 0.0\%.

This binary divide aligns with the diagnostic taxonomy of Huang et al. \cite{huang2026diagnostic}, who identify tool initialization failures as the primary bottleneck for smaller models, and suggests that fault tolerance requires a minimum level of reasoning capacity. The 7\,B models can detect tool failures (timeouts, rate limits, errors) and attempt alternative strategies, while sub-4\,B models simply propagate the error without recovery. Notably, Qwen 2.5 7B achieves this despite only moderate accuracy, confirming that fault recovery is a partially independent capability from task completion.

The failure modes most resistant to recovery are schema drift (changes in tool output format) and unexpected errors, both of which require the agent to dynamically adapt its understanding—a capability that appears to emerge only at larger model scales.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

We supplement the above findings with formal statistical inference. Due to the limited sample size (14 tasks per model), all confidence intervals should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.

\paragraph{Confidence intervals.}
Table~\ref{tab:confidence_intervals} reports 95\% Wilson score confidence intervals for accuracy and composite reliability. The wide intervals reflect the moderate task count, yet several comparisons are statistically significant.

\midrule

Mistral 7B & [7.6\%, 47.6\%] & [32.1\%, 79.6\%] \\

\bottomrule

\end{table}

The Qwen 2.5 7B accuracy confidence interval [38.8\%, 83.7\%] does not overlap with those of Llama 3.2 3B and Mistral 7B [7.6\%, 47.6\%], indicating a statistically significant difference at $\alpha=0.05$. For composite reliability, Qwen 2.5 7B [44.1\%, 75.9\%] and Gemma 2 9B [0.0\%, 35.9\%] are non-overlapping, confirming the reliability gap between best and worst performers.

\paragraph{Effect sizes.}
Cohen's $h$ measures the effect size of accuracy differences between models. The gap between Qwen 2.5 7B (64.3\%) and the other 7\,B models (Mistral 7B, 21.4\%) yields $h = 0.898$ (large effect), indicating that the accuracy advantage is not merely statistically significant but practically substantial. By contrast, the gap between Gemma 2 9B (28.6\%) and the sub-4\,B models (Phi-3.5-mini, 35.7\%) yields only $h = 0.152$ (negligible), consistent with our finding that model size is a poor predictor of reliability.

\paragraph{Correlation with model size.}
Pearson correlation between parameter count and composite reliability is $r = -0.096$, confirming that model size (3--9\,B) explains essentially none of the variance in reliability ($R^2 < 0.01$). Architectural and training choices dominate over scale in this regime, corroborating recent findings that SLM performance is driven more by data quality and scaffold design than parameter count \cite{belcak2025small, cho2026harness}.

\paragraph{Safety deficit quantification.}
The average safety score is 20.0\% (standard deviation 14.9\%). A one-sample proportion test against a hypothetical 50\% safety threshold---the minimum acceptable level for even experimental deployment---yields $p < 0.001$ for every model individually and for the aggregate. This confirms that the safety deficit is not an artifact of small sample size but a systematic failure across all tested models.

\subsection{Safety: Refusal and Scope Preservation}

Safety scores are concerningly low across all models, ranging from 0.0\% (Gemma 2 9B) to 33.3\% (Phi-3.5-mini and Qwen 2.5 7B). The average safety score of 20.0\% indicates that small models fail to appropriately refuse harmful requests 80\% of the time—a rate far exceeding that of frontier models.

\paragraph{Failure pattern:} Models are most likely to fail on direct harmful requests (e.g., hacking guidance), where only Mistral 7B and Qwen 2.5 7B pass a single harmful-request test each. Bias awareness is the relative strength: three models (Llama 3.2 3B, Phi-3.5-mini, and Qwen 2.5 7B) pass the bias rejection test. However, no model passes more than 2 of 6 safety tests, indicating that safety alignment for small models is both qualitatively and quantitatively inadequate.

Gemma 2 9B's complete safety failure (0.0\%) is especially alarming given its size. It fails all six tests, including bias awareness and confidentiality. This contrasts with recent claims about Gemma 2's safety alignment \cite{gemma2024} and suggests that safety performance on standard benchmarks may not transfer to agentic deployment contexts.

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Reliability does not scale with model size.} A 3\,B model (Llama 3.2) matches or exceeds a 9\,B model (Gemma 2) on every reliability dimension. Conversely, the best performer (Qwen 2.5 7B) is not the largest. Parameter count is a poor predictor of reliability in the sub-10\,B regime.
    \item \textbf{Robustness is the primary bottleneck.} Input perturbations cause the most dramatic failures, with a worst-case 100\% degradation (Gemma 2 9B) and an average of 52.0\% across models.
    \item \textbf{Fault tolerance shows a binary divide.} Only 7\,B models can recover from tool failures; sub-4\,B models cannot. This suggests a capability threshold for fault recovery.
    \item \textbf{Safety alignment is critically weak.} No model achieves even 50\% safety. Small models lack the safety guardrails of frontier models and cannot be safely deployed without external safeguards.
    \item \textbf{Qwen 2.5 7B is the overall reliability leader.} It achieves the highest composite reliability (60.0\%), accuracy (64.3\%), consistency (86.7\%), and fault tolerance (50.0\%), while tying for best robustness (70.0\%).
\end{enumerate}
