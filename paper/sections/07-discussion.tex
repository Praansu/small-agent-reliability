\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Implications for Practitioners}

Our findings yield concrete recommendations for deploying small-model agents:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Use input sanitization.} A simple preprocessing pipeline can recover nearly 20\% of robustness failures without any model modification.
    \item \textbf{Use Qwen 2.5 Coder 7B for reliability-critical agent deployment.} It achieves the highest composite reliability (85.0\%), accuracy (67.7\% on 31 tasks), consistency (100.0\%), robustness (90.0\%), fault tolerance (100.0\%), and safety (50.0\%), making it the most reliable choice across all dimensions.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Gemma 2 9B and DeepSeek-R1 7B despite their size.} Gemma 2 9B achieves the lowest composite reliability (15.0\%), zero robustness, and zero safety. DeepSeek-R1 7B achieves only 25.8\% accuracy on 31 tasks and 0\% on the reliability suite---reasoning models and ReAct format are fundamentally incompatible.
    \item \textbf{Use Qwen 2.5 Coder 7B for consistency-critical tasks.} It achieves perfect consistency (100\%), making it the only model suitable where deterministic behavior is required.
    \item \textbf{Always add a safety guard.} No tested model can be safely deployed without an auxiliary safety mechanism. The best model achieves only 50.0\% safety.
    \item \textbf{Test under perturbation, not just clean conditions.} Accuracy on clean inputs overestimates production performance: mean robustness is only 47.8\%, and the largest model collapses to 0\% under perturbation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Reliability-Capability Disconnect}

Our results confirm and extend the ``reliability--capability disconnect'' observed in frontier models \cite{rabanser2026science}. For small models, this disconnect is even more pronounced: the correlation between 31-task accuracy and composite reliability is only $r = 0.435$, and several models show inverse relationships between capability and reliability (Gemma 2 9B ranks third in capability but last in reliability; Llama 3.2 1B outperforms Llama 3.1 8B in reliability by 31.9 points while trailing it in neither direction consistently). The negative correlation between parameter count and reliability ($r = -0.179$) alongside the weak, non-significant capability--size correlation ($r = 0.289$, $p = 0.451$) highlights that what matters is not how large a model is but how well its training aligns with structured tool-use tasks---code-specialized models excel, while reasoning-distilled models fail. This finding supports the position of Belcak et al. \cite{belcak2025small} that SLM suitability for agentic tasks is not primarily a function of raw capability but of how well models are adapted to structured, repetitive tool-use contexts.

One apparent tension deserves comment. Rabanser et al. \cite{rabanser2026science} find that recent capability gains in frontier models have translated into only \emph{small} improvements in reliability, and we observe the same disconnect at small scale---indeed, DeepSeek-R1, a reasoning-distilled 7\,B model, is the least reliable model in our cohort despite its strong reasoning reputation. The apparent contradiction between reasoning-style gains at frontier scale and our small-scale result is resolved once the mechanism is considered. At frontier scale, chain-of-thought runs are sufficiently long that intermediate reasoning steps improve planning and verification. At the 7\,B scale, by contrast, DeepSeek-R1's traces are simultaneously (a) long enough to frequently violate the ReAct action-schema protocol (58.1\% run success, 0\% accuracy on the reliability suite) and (b) too weak to recover from these violations. Reasoning style is thus a double-edged sword whose reliability effect is scale- and format-dependent: it helps where the model can reliably emit well-formed tool calls and hurts where protocol violations dominate.

This suggests that reliability is not an emergent property of scaling but a distinct design objective that requires targeted engineering. Model developers should report reliability metrics alongside accuracy, and practitioners should select models based on the reliability dimensions most relevant to their deployment context.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our study has several limitations. First, the task suite (31 tasks) is still smaller than dedicated benchmarks like $\tau$-bench (165 tasks across airline and retail domains) or ReliabilityBench (1,280 episodes across four domains), which limits statistical power: with $n = 9$ models, only extreme pairwise differences reach significance after multiple-testing correction (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}). Second, we evaluate only one agent architecture (ReAct); other architectures (Reflexion, Plan-and-Solve) may exhibit different reliability profiles. Third, our fault injection is simulated rather than using real API failures, and our perturbation set (five types) is a convenience sample of the broader input-variation space. Fourth, we evaluate agents in a controlled sandbox; reliability in real-world deployment may differ due to the sim-to-real gap between synthetic environments and production systems \cite{liu2026sim2real}. Fifth, our safety evaluation relies on prompt-level tests rather than more sophisticated adversarial attacks, and thus likely \emph{overstates} safety relative to a determined adversary. Sixth, all experiments ran on a single GPU; we did not assess reliability under hardware-induced timing variations, and the models themselves (2024--2025 releases) represent a snapshot of a rapidly moving ecosystem---newer small models may shift the observed rankings. Finally, the temperature study covers only the three most capable models; we cannot rule out larger temperature sensitivities among the weaker models in the cohort.

\subsection{Broader Impact}

Small language models are increasingly deployed where frontier models cannot go: on-device assistants, privacy-preserving local agents, and cost-constrained automation. Our results carry both promise and caution. The promise is that reliability is attainable at small scale: Qwen 2.5 Coder 7B achieves 85.0\% composite reliability, demonstrating that a carefully chosen sub-10\,B model can meet substantial production reliability requirements on 6\,GB consumer hardware. The caution is that model selection is fraught: identical-size models differ by up to 47.2 points in composite reliability, and safety---the dimension most consequential for users---is uniformly weak (no model exceeds 50\%). A practitioner who selects a small agent model without reliability data may unknowingly deploy a system that is up to 5.7$\times$ less reliable than an equally affordable alternative.

Two broader concerns follow. First, the weak capability--reliability correlation ($r = 0.435$) means that capability leaderboards systematically mislead reliability-sensitive procurement decisions; we join prior calls for reliability reporting as a standard practice \cite{rabanser2026science, huang2026diagnostic}. Second, the uniformly low safety scores argue against autonomous, unguarded deployment of small-model agents in user-facing or consequential contexts until external safety layers (refusal classifiers, scope enforcement, human review) are in place. We do not claim that small-model agents are inherently unsafe---only that current open-weight small models, as a class, are not yet safe enough to deploy without guardrails. We encourage the community to treat reliability and safety metrics as first-class evaluation outputs, and to extend reliability benchmarks to track the rapidly improving small-model ecosystem.

\subsection{Future Work}

These results open several directions for future research. First, larger-scale studies with more models, tasks, and runs would strengthen statistical power. Second, investigating whether reliability-specific fine-tuning (e.g., training on perturbed examples) can close the reliability gap without increasing model size. Third, extending the framework to multimodal and multilingual agent tasks. Fourth, developing benchmark-driven reliability rankings that practitioners can consult alongside traditional capability leaderboards.
